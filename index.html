Introduction

Motivation

The hierarchical structure of collegiate competition largely reflects the stratified social and economic dimensions of the communities from which colleges draw their students. Competition among colleges, as admissions officers have told us for so long, is in fact, a matter of keeping company with one’s peers. — The Structure of College Choice (Zemsky & Oedel, 1983)

Social closure: status groups restrict access to resources that confer privilege

  • Selective colleges are site of closure
    • Privileged groups redefine admissions criteria to benefit themselves

But colleges don’t passively accept applicants; they recruit

  • If recruiting markets constructed by shared infrastructures
  • Then social closure operates upstream of admissions

Do College Board Geomarkets structure high school recruiting visits by selective colleges?

Sociology of Education

How are students sorted into colleges?

Explanations from Annual Review of Sociology

  • Households: status attainment, cultural capital
  • Schools: “sorting machines” that assign categories (e.g., tracks)
  • Colleges
    • Selective admissions: which criteria ration scarce seats

Colleges looking for patrons (Stevens and Gebre-Medhin 2016)

  • In “enrollment economy,” stability depends on enrollment (Clark 1956)
    • Responsibility of “enrollment management” (Stevens 2007; Hossler 1984)
    • High school recruiting visits (Salazar, Jaquette, and Han 2021)

Third-party intermediaries “occupy the interface between consumers and producers” (Sharkey, Kovács, and Hsu 2023, 1)

  • Sociology studies consumer-facing intermediaries (e.g., rankings)
  • But business-facing intermediaries sort students on behalf of colleges

Case

The Structure of College Choice (Zemsky and Oedel 1983)

Project goal: “capture and quantify” (p. 11) knowledge of admissions officers

Creates three geographic levels: region, state, community (Geomarket)

  • Geomarkets carve states and metro areas into smaller geographic regions

Create 4 student “market segments” based on SAT score-sending behavior

  • local, in-state, regional, national
  • local: send most scores to colleges within Geomarket
  • regional: send most scores out-of-state but in-region (e.g., Midwest)

Market Segment Model predicts how demand for a college varies by Geomarket

  • Student market segment mostly a function of class
  • Selective colleges primarily enroll “regional” and “national” students
  • Most “regional” and “national” students are located in affluent Geomarkets
  • Selective colleges should focus recruiting on affluent Geomarkets

Theory

Market devices and classification situations

Market devices literature (Callon 1998; Muniesa, Millo, and Callon 2007)

  • Market devices: tools that calculate/classify, then get incorporated in products
    • Geomarkets: defines recruiting territories
    • Market Segment Model: theory is abstract idea, until embedded in a device
      • Enrollment Planning Service (EPS) software
  • Agencement: devices + org roles/goals produce menu of possible/likely actions
    • Colleges commensurate Geomarkets based on market segments, SES, scores
  • Performativity: Usage of devices produce behavior consistent with theory
    • Here: colleges use Geomarkets + related devices to produce recruiting behavior consistent with Market Segment Model

Classification Situations (Fourcade and Healy 2013, 2024)

  • Ordinally ranked consumers matched to ordinally ranked products
  • Market Segment Model: ordinally ranked student segments (local, in-state, regional, national) matched to ordinally ranked colleges

Theory

Research questions and expectations

RQ1: Are visit patterns consistent with Market Segment Model recommendations?

  • Expectation: For selective privates and most publics in sample, Market Segment Model recommends focusing on affluent Geomarkets in regions where many students likely to consider a college like yours

RQ2: Do Geomarkets (X) explain which high schools receive recruiting visits (Y)

  • Geomarkets embedded in org + tech infrastructure that produces visits
    • Colleges assign admissions officers to Geomarkets (LinkedIn profiles)
    • Geomarkets + Market Segment Model basis for Enrollment Planning Service
      • Identifies Geomarkets with high demand for peer/competitor colleges
    • Geomarkets integrated into Slate/Oracle CRM software

RQ3: Does Geomarket popularity (number of visits per school) mediate relationship between school composition (SES/race) and probability of a receiving a visit?

  • Classification situations seek “good matches” between consumers and products
  • A wealthy school in a wealthy Geomarket is a good match

Methods

Data and sample

Data collection (Salazar, Jaquette, and Han 2021)

  • Public “research-extensive” universities from 2000 Carnegie (N=102)

  • Assign Census tracts to Geomarkets by spatial overlay of College Board (2023)

RQ2: Do Geomarkets explain which schools get visits?

Fixed effects linear probability models of visits to public schools

Single-college model of visits to school \(i\)

\[ Y_i = \alpha + X_i \beta + \gamma_{g(i)} + \varepsilon_i \]

  • \(Y_i = 1\) if high school \(i\) received \(\geq 1\) recruiting visit.
  • \(X_i\): school- and neighborhood-level covariates
    • School: g12 enrollment, race/ethnicity, Niche quality grade, free/reduced lunch, math & reading proficiency, 0/1 magnet, distance school \(i\) to college \(j\)
    • ZIP code: household income, BA+ attainment, poverty, race/ethnicity
  • \(\gamma_{g(i)}\): fixed effect for geographic unit (e.g., Geomarket) containing school \(i\)

School-college pair model, visits to school \(i\) by college \(j\)

\[ Y_{ij} = \alpha + X_i \beta + \gamma_{j \times g(i)} + \varepsilon_{ij} \]

  • \(\gamma_{j \times g(i)}\): college-specific geographic fixed effects
    • Equal to dummy vars for each combo of college \(j\) and geographic unit \(g(i)\)

Discussion

Contribution to scholarship

Main findings

  • Consistent with Market Segment Model, most colleges followed regional/national recruiting strategy that prioritized affluent Geomarkets
  • Geomarkets explain which high schools receive visits
  • Affluent, moderately diverse schools benefit from being in a popular Geomarket

Contribution

  • Dominant explanations of selective college access focuses on admissions
    • college-level (Karabel 2005); macro forces (e.g., rankings, affirmative action)
  • The literature needs to move upstream in two ways, simultaneously
    1. In process, from admissions to recruiting (Stevens 2007)
    2. In actors, from colleges to third-party market devices that colleges rely on
  • Third-party recruiting infrastructure is a mechanism of social closure
    • Shapes which students get pursued by which colleges
    • Can be usefully studied as a set of interacting market devices

Discussion

Infrastructural collusion

In Zemsky and Oedel (1983) chap. 4, “The Company We Keep: Colleges and Their Competition,” two colleges are competitors if many students send scores to both:

In the words of one veteran dean of admissions, ‘We are known primarily by the company we keep.’ For this reason, colleges actually encourage competition with one another to define their places in the market. Members of the Consortium on Financing Higher Education (COFHE) – a group of thirty highly selective, high-cost, private institutions – regularly exchange detailed and confidential information about their applicant pools, admissions strategies, and financial aid programs.

What every envious admissions officer outside the Consortium understands is that these thirty institutions are strengthened by their collectivity, though they are in fact one another’s principal competitors. The dean of admissions at one COFHE institution knows that there is greater chance of interesting a student who is already considering another COFHE institution than a student who is not.

The same kind of forces help define collective markets for state colleges, women’s colleges, Catholic or other church-related institutions, and even diverse urban institutions that share intense athletic rivalries (p. 45).

Discussion

Infrastructural collusion

Peer-patronage logic: Zemsky and Oedel (1983) observe that college reputation is joint function of (a) social class of students and (b) peer colleges it associates with

  • Socioeconomic homophily: like colleges compete for students of like social class

Building this logic into devices, we go from observing to structuring competition

  • College Board Enrollment Planning Service (EPS) “competitive analysis” tab:
    • Locate Geomarkets that showed strong demand for peer colleges
  • By relying on shared infrastructure, competitors coordinate without collusion

Engineering homophily in social networks (Chun 2021):

  • Identify variables that predict connections
  • Write algorithms that makes it easy/default to create those connections
  • College-facing recruiting products have “interest in my peers” type filter
  • Consulting firms (EAB), member assoc (College Board) serve 100s of colleges
    • Incentive to match “right” students to each college (Fourcade and Healy 2013)

Discussion

Future research on market devices in edtech

Three ideal-type origins of third-party market devices in enrollment management

  1. Built by (elite) higher ed insiders, based on logic of higher ed insiders
    • Example: Geomarkets, Market Segment Model, and EPS
  2. Higher ed insiders apply commercial marketing logic/techniques
    • Example: College Board (2011) Geodemographic Segment Analysis Service
  3. Private equity firms applying software-as-service models to higher ed
    • Example: EAB’s Enroll360 (Koppenheffer 2021)

Future research

  • A critical historical sociology of enrollment management industry
    • Logical starting point is the College Board
  • Sociology (and economics) has not made edtech an object of inquiry
    • Useful concepts: infrastructures (Star 1999); market devices
    • “Platform studies in education” literature: how digital platforms organize core functions of schooling (Nichols and Garcia 2022)
