**P5 — proposed:**

Sociology has often defined social class from the production side of the economy, for example through ownership and occupation. By contrast, @RN4810 argue that processes of ordinalization "endogenous to the market" (p. 560) define class through effects on the consumption side of the economy. Classification situations are positions in markets produced when organizations "sort and slot people into new types of categories ... with different economic rewards or punishments attached to them" [@RN4810, p. 561]. Organizations seek "good matches" between ordinally classified products and consumers, aiming to link "a wealthy customer with a quality credit card, or a heavy drinker with a bad insurance plan" [@RN4808, p. 17]. These matches capitalize on socially patterned dispositions and practical habits [@RN4808, p. 17; @RN1228]. Homophily can operate through these mutually reinforcing status associations: products acquire status through their consumers, while consumers signal and reinforce their status through what they consume [@RN1237; @RN1228]. Matching can thereby reproduce hierarchies while making them appear natural.

**P6 — proposed:**

A place's classification may supply expectations about the people or organizations within it, so treatment may depend on whether a particular actor fits those expectations. @RN4808 [pp. 20–21] describe an episode in which one of them had a credit-card transaction denied at a gas station in a high-poverty Oakland neighborhood. The clerk suggested that the location explained the denial; shortly afterward, the same card worked in Berkeley. The episode illustrates how a person's apparent fit with a classified context can become consequential for treatment. An affluent school with predominantly White or Asian enrollment in an affluent Geomarket may be perceived as a good match because it exemplifies the schools recruiters expect to find. By contrast, schools whose socioeconomic or racial composition departs from those expectations may not share equally in the recruiting attention directed toward the Geomarket.

**Revised P4 — proposed wording, not yet accepted or pasted:**

Theories (e.g., pricing theory, status attainment theory), by themselves, are not market devices. They are abstract ideas that can be applied to specific contexts. For example, homophily is the idea that people who share similar characteristics are more likely to connect [@mcpherson2001birds]. Theories become part of market devices when components of the theory are embedded within the device. Performativity is the idea that theories of market behavior do not merely describe behavior; rather, market devices are constructed and acted upon in ways that bring about behavior consistent with the theory [@RN5064; @RN5067]. @RN4975 problematizes the idea that homophily is a naturally occurring phenomenon. Actors within particular social structures, including elite institutional networks, design devices around specific theories and understandings of market behavior. These assumptions can therefore orient subsequent users toward behavior consistent with the devices' theoretical origins.

Commensuration is "the expression or measurement -- of characteristics normally represented by different units -- according to a common metric" [@RN5063, p. 315]. **P1 — proposed, not yet accepted or known to be inserted:**

The market devices literature examines how the components of markets are assembled and enacted, and how these processes shape behavior [@callon1998embeddedness; @RN5064; @RN5067]. Market devices are tools for calculating, comparing, and classifying (e.g., algorithms, metrics, rankings) that become incorporated into products and transactions "that make economic action possible" [@RN4777, p. 1176]. @RN5064 [p. 2] define market devices as a "way of referring to the material and discursive assemblages that intervene in the construction of markets." Here, material refers to tools and technologies, like spreadsheets or software, while discursive refers to representational elements, such as categorical, quantitative, and ordinal variables. An assemblage refers to a bundle of heterogeneous components that work together, suggesting that market devices consist of and build upon other market devices. Market devices emerge from the collective action of socially embedded networks of people and organizations [@RN5067; @mullerseitz2012maneuvering].
